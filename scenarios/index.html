Emergency? Call 911

They Won't Wake Up

Practice recognizing a possible opioid overdose, giving naloxone, and getting help. This is a fictional scenario for education.

A friend lies unresponsive on the ground. One student kneels beside them with a hand on their shoulder and calls 911 on a phone. Another student stands nearby holding a naloxone nasal spray.

Step 1 of 3

They won't wake up.

After school, your friend is slumped near the courtyard. You shout their name and shake their shoulder, but they don't respond. Their breathing is slow and unusual. Nobody knows what happened.

What should you do first?

Sources and how they were used
  • CDC: What to Do If You Think Someone Is Overdosing — The overall sequence: naloxone and 911, keep them awake and breathing, lay them on their side, stay until help arrives.
  • CDC: Naloxone FAQ — Naloxone works within 2–3 minutes; wait 2–3 minutes before a second dose.
  • FDA label and Instructions for Use: NARCAN Nasal Spray (DailyMed) — Device steps: lay on back, tilt head, nozzle in one nostril, press firmly, no priming or testing; recovery position; second dose from a new device in the other nostril.
  • CDC/NIOSH: Naloxone use in a suspected overdose — Naloxone effects are temporary; rescue breathing or CPR if trained.
  • NYS Department of Health: How to Inject Naloxone — Checking for a response (shout, shake) and signs such as gurgling sounds and blue or gray lips.
  • Alameda County Health: Opioid Treatment and Support — Naloxone is free and over the counter; stand boxes; training for the public and youth.
  • Alameda County Health: First Step — Phone number, hours, and that it is not a crisis line.
  • California Health & Safety Code §11376.5 — 911 Good Samaritan protection for personal-use possession and being under the influence.
